A sanctions list is a signal, not a wall.

Governments name companies. Banks, exporters, shippers and insurers do the actual stopping. Evaders work in the gap between the two. This page explains the machine, the tricks, and how our team tracked them in real trade data.

1 · The machine

How sanctions work

A sanction is a government rule that says: do not do business with this person, company or country. It is a way to put pressure on someone without firing a shot.

Who imposes them

Worldwide

UN Security Council

Can require every UN member to freeze assets, ban travel and embargo arms. Most often it targets named people and groups.1

United States

Treasury (OFAC)

The Office of Foreign Assets Control keeps the SDN list (the main US blacklist). Anyone on it is cut off from US dollars and US firms.3

United States

Commerce (BIS)

The Bureau of Industry and Security runs export controls. Its Entity List means: you need a licence to send US-controlled goods to this company, or even to this address.8

UK and EU

OFSI and the EU Council

The UK's Office of Financial Sanctions Implementation (part of HM Treasury) enforces asset freezes. The EU adopts sanctions that each member state enforces.9,10

The main tools

  • Asset freeze (also called blocking). Nobody may pay, hold or move the target's money or property.9
  • Export controls. Sensitive goods need a licence, which is usually refused for listed buyers.8
  • Sector bans. Whole kinds of trade are cut, such as buying Russian oil above a price cap.9
  • Ownership rules. Companies owned by a listed party can be blocked without being named (section 2).3
  • Secondary sanctions (pressure on outsiders). Since Executive Order 14114 (December 2023), the US can sanction a foreign bank that handles significant deals for Russia's military industry.5,6 Foreign banks now have their own reason to check.
  • A battlefield-goods list. The US, EU, UK and Japan share the Common High Priority List: 50 product types, by customs code, found in Russian missiles and drones. Chips (integrated circuits) are in the top tier.7
  • Humanitarian exemptions. Since December 2022, food, medicine and aid are carved out of all UN asset freezes, and the US issues matching general licences.2,11

How a sanction actually bites

Governments rarely stop a shipment themselves. The list works because private gatekeepers check it and refuse: a bank will not move the money, an exporter will not sell, a shipper will not carry, an insurer will not cover. Each of them faces fines if they get it wrong. So the list is a signal. If a gatekeeper never sees the listed name, the signal never arrives.

2 · The moving parts

What's involved

Every sanctions case is built from the same seven pieces. Evasion means changing one piece so the others stop lining up.

Goods

What is traded, identified by a customs product code (codes starting 8542 are chips).

Routes

Which countries the paperwork says the goods left and entered.

Companies

The shipper, the buyer, and any middlemen. Their names are what lists match on.

Ownership

Who owns whom, and by how much. Decides who is blocked without being named.

Lists

Several, run by different governments, which do not always agree.

Time

When a company appeared, when it traded, and when it was finally listed.

Observers

Customs, banks, data companies, journalists. Each sees only part of the picture.

The 50% rule: blocked without being named

In the US, any company owned 50% or more in total by listed parties is blocked automatically, even through a chain of other companies. Stakes from different listed owners are added together.3,4 The EU's test is slightly different: more than 50%, or "control", such as the power to appoint most of the board.10 The line is sharp, so where a company sits relative to it matters a lot.

3 · The tricks

How countries and companies evade

Almost every trick does the same thing: it breaks the match between a listed name and a transaction. Below are six, each with an example from our own work or an official source.

  1. 1. Route through a third country

    Goods that can no longer go straight to Russia are sold to a neighbour first, then passed on.

    Our data: countries' own reports to the UN show chip exports to Russia jumping from almost nothing. Kyrgyzstan: about $0 a year in 2019 to 2021, then $13.5M in 2023. Kazakhstan: $18.3M in 2022. Armenia: $13.1M in 2022.12

  1. 2. Set up front companies, then replace them

    New firms with no history do the trading. When one gets noticed, another takes over.

    Official example: the US Treasury's own entry for RM Design and Development (Kyrgyzstan) says it was established on 17 March 2022, 21 days after the invasion. The US listed it in July 2023.14

  2. 3. Stay just under 50%, or hide a company in the middle

    A stake under the line is not blocked automatically, and an unnamed subsidiary does not show up in a name search.

  3. 4. Relabel the goods

    Describe a controlled item as something harmless, or file it under a different product code.

    Official example: in April 2026 the US Treasury described a broker shipping fentanyl ingredients labelled as "safe chemicals".16

  4. 5. Transfer at sea and fly a false flag

    Oil is pumped from ship to ship out at sea, and tankers register under flags they are not entitled to.

    Official and research examples: 113 tankers under false flags carried €4.7bn of Russian oil in the first nine months of 2025.17 One tanker in our data changed its name five times.

  5. 6. Trade before the list catches up

    Listing a company takes an investigation. Until then, trading is easy.

    Official example: RM Design and Development was listed on 20 July 2023, at least 16 months after the founding date in its own US Treasury entry.14 ELEM GROUP (Kazakhstan) went on a US export blacklist in December 2023 and the US sanctions list in February 2024.13

4 · The response

How we can track them

Track the need, not the name. A name is the easiest thing to change. What Russia's weapons industry needs (a specific chip, delivered to a specific factory) changes slowly. So we watch the product, the buyer, the route, and treat company names as a lagging label.

The methods

  1. Triangulate sources. We combine official listing pages and government releases with countries' own official statistics (UN Comtrade) and, for company links, a sponsor's trade-link records (Tradeverifyd). A claim stands only when two independent sources agree. Comtrade is how we see the middle countries' chip exports to Russia jump after 2022.
  2. Alias and ID cross-checks. Match tax numbers and registration IDs, not just names: a name check alone misses transliterated and shortened names.
  3. Sibling finder. Look for unlisted firms sharing an address and a trading partner with a listed one. On the US list we found 240 pairs of Russia-programme companies at the same address that were listed in separate waves, a median of about 5.5 months apart. Busy addresses (registration agents, business towers) count for less.
  4. Listing-lag monitoring. Start a clock at a firm's first suspicious sign (registration, first shipment) and watch who is still trading with it after it is listed.
  5. Weapon-part tracing. Ukraine's military intelligence (GUR) publishes parts found in Russian weapons. It lists an STM32F765VIT6 microcontroller in the Shahed-136 attack drone.15 Searching trade records for the full part number, not the maker's name, leads from a weapon to the firms that ship that exact part. A part found in a weapon never proves that a particular shipment reached one.
  6. A built-in skeptic. A separate checker re-derives every number from the saved raw data before it is shown. It caught doubled totals (an Armenia figure of $52M that was really $13.1M) and a database start date posing as a listing date.

What the data can't see

  • Countries' own statistics show totals by country, not which companies shipped.
  • Ship-to-ship transfers and relabelled oil.
  • Cash deals, smuggling, and goods moved inside a country.
  • Whether a shipped chip ended up in a weapon.
  • Being unlisted, or absent from our data, does not mean a company is clean.

5 · The stakes

Why it matters, and the ethics

The parts that keep drones and missiles flying are ordinary commercial chips. Closing the gaps in Diagram 1 is how sanctions stop being paperwork. But a tool that points at companies can also hurt the wrong people, so we hold three rules.

Leads, not findings

A path in the data is a reason to look, not proof of wrongdoing. We only say a company is sanctioned when an official list says so. Unlisted companies our tools surface go to investigators privately, never onto a slide.

Precision, not panic

When gatekeepers are unsure, the cheap answer is to refuse everyone in a region. That falls on families. After US sanctions on Iran returned in 2018, medicine exports to Iran (as partner countries report them) fell by about 35%, from $1.30bn in 2017 to $0.84bn in 2023, even though medicine is meant to be exempt. Medicine exports to Russia stayed flat, about $12bn a year.12 In 2015 a US bank closed the accounts that carried money home to Somalia, where such transfers are 25% to 45% of the economy.18 Good targeting means naming the few firms that matter, not cutting off a country.

Who pays if a flag is wrong

A false match lands on a real business: big office towers hold hundreds of companies, a few of them listed. Sharing a building is not evidence. So every lead needs two independent signals, shows its evidence rather than a score, and says what the data could not check.
